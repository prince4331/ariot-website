## The import-first assumption

When a Bangladesh company wants to automate a factory task or deploy an IoT network, the default
assumption is to import the solution. German robots. Chinese sensors. US software platforms.

This assumption has a cost that rarely appears in the procurement spreadsheet:

- **High import duties** that inflate the landed cost above the sticker price.
- **Long lead times** — spare parts that arrive in 3 months are not useful when a machine is down.
- **Limited local support** — the vendor's nearest service engineer may be in Singapore or Dubai.
- **Hardware designed for a different market** — power profiles, thermal ranges, dust ratings, and
  operator skill assumptions all calibrated for a customer who is not you.

None of this means imported solutions are wrong. Some categories have no local alternative yet.
But the assumption that import is always the right default deserves more scrutiny than it gets.

## What local engineering unlocks

A locally-engineered solution changes several things:

**Support economics**: When something breaks, the engineer who designed the firmware is reachable.
Not via a 12-hour timezone gap and a ticket queue — by phone or in person.

**Duty cycle alignment**: The hardware is designed around how it will actually be used, not around
the average use case for a global market. If a factory floor runs a 22-hour duty cycle with a
30-minute maintenance window, the control board is designed for that. Not retrofitted.

**Spare parts availability**: Components are sourced to be available locally or from regional
distributors with short lead times. A main board failure is a one-week fix, not a two-month wait.

**Cost path**: The cost structure for local production is different. Lower labour costs offset the
absence of economies of scale in early production runs. As volume grows, the gap closes further.

## The skills gap is real but closeable

Bangladesh has a growing embedded engineering community. Its universities and training institutes produce engineers who can read schematics, write firmware, and assemble PCBs. The gap is not in
raw talent.

The gap is in:

- **Exposure to hardware product development cycles** — prototype to production to field support.
  Most engineers learn to build things. Fewer learn to build things that ship and get maintained.
- **Access to modern toolchains** — professional PCB layout tools, version-controlled firmware
  workflows, and proper test infrastructure are not yet standard in the local ecosystem.
- **The culture of treating hardware as a product** — with documentation, user-facing errors, and
  support paths built in from day one, not added at launch when they are expensive to retrofit.

ARIOT exists to close this gap from the inside. Every product we build is an argument that it is
possible. Every engineer we work with learns a production cycle, not just a lab exercise.
